Speed Is the Moat

A moat is not an advantage. It is an advantage a competitor cannot cross.

No. The dispersion in inference speed is large and real, but a lead inside it has a measured half-life of one to two months, latency is already sold as a contract tier rather than won, and the one controlled experiment on interactive latency found faster responses changed no behaviour and were judged worse.

01The premise needs one correction

Cost is understood — 98% of 1,192 State of FinOps respondents manage AI spend, up from 31% two years earlier. Cost is not settled: it is still where frontier vendors compete in public.

More importantly, speed and cost are not sequential axes. They are two readings of one batch-size curve. On fixed hardware you cannot have both minimum latency and maximum throughput, and where a provider sits between them is a commercial decision as much as a technical one.

03Why “moat” is the wrong word

The optimisations that once separated a fast serving stack from a slow one — chunked prefill, prefix caching, speculative decoding, disaggregated prefill/decode — are now standard features of open-source vLLM.

An independent benchmark puts a number on the erosion: AMD's inference software improved up to 2× between December 2025 and January 2026, and nearly doubled throughput at equal interactivity in under two months — on hardware that did not change.

A lead that erodes on that cadence is shorter-lived than the procurement cycle meant to capture it.

04The interactive case is weaker than assumed

The most-cited evidence that latency costs money — Amazon's "every 100ms costs 1% of sales" — is an e-commerce page-load result from a different era and a different interaction, recycled into AI latency marketing.

The direct test says something else. A CHI 2026 controlled experiment varied time-to-first-token across 2, 9 and 20 seconds for 240 participants. Interaction behaviour was robust to latency. Perceived quality ran backwards: 2-second responses were rated less thoughtful and less useful than 9–20-second ones, with participants reading delay as deliberation.

One experiment, one band, one task family. But within what it tested, the best available evidence contradicts the folklore it replaces.

05Where speed does pay

Throughput and agentic wall-clock — and it is purchasable, not won. Batch APIs at both OpenAI and Anthropic charge 50% of standard price for a 24-hour window. Identical work, identical model, half the budget, seconds becoming a day.

Agentic workloads multiply sequential calls per task — tens of turns typically, up to 200. That multiplication is the mechanism converting a per-token rate into hours of wall clock, and it is why the question is sharper now than it was for single-turn chat.

And latency is already governed at the platform layer. Microsoft's deployment-type documentation carries an explicit Latency SLA column: Priority and Provisioned carry a defined target, Standard and Batch carry none. Latency is not an unclaimed strategic axis that leaders failed to notice. It is a priced contract term, and most organisations are on the ungoverned tier by default rather than by decision.

06What to do

  1. Classify workloads by tier before comparing vendors. The batch/standard/priority split holds the largest wall-clock difference available for a fixed budget, without changing provider.
  2. Treat open-weight vs proprietary as the gate. Speed shopping pays 5–8× on open weights and roughly nothing on proprietary models.
  3. Buy the latency you need and stop paying for the rest. A latency target is a contract term you can already request; on the standard tier there is nothing to enforce.
  4. Do not assume faster is better in the interface. Spend the latency budget where it compounds — agentic loops — not on shaving an interactive response users may not reward.

Where the phrase came from

"Speed is the moat" appears in this corpus as a quote from Anush Elangovan, VP GPU Software at AMD, in a benchmark launch post. Nvidia's framing of the same benchmark stresses performance per dollar and per megawatt.

When the two largest GPU vendors describe identical results and one says speed while the other says efficiency, the framing is positioning, not physics.
